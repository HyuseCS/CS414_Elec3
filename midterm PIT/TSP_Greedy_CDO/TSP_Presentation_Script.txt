02 • Real-world problem (0:55)
ON SCREEN: Point to the tour scenario and the requirement to return home.

Imagine a visitor who wants to photograph and document several landmarks around downtown Cagayan de Oro. The visitor starts at Gaston Park, visits every required destination once, and returns to Gaston Park. The question is: in what order should the visitor make these stops to reduce the total distance? This is a form of the Traveling Salesperson Problem. In our implementation, the objective is to minimize the total modeled distance of a closed tour.

04 • Graph and assumptions (1:10)
ON SCREEN: Explain complete, undirected and why these are proxies.

A weighted graph consists of vertices, edges and weights. In our graph, vertices represent landmarks. Edges represent a possible direct transition from one landmark to another, and weights are distances in kilometers. We chose a complete, undirected graph, meaning any pair is connected and the distance from A to B equals the distance from B to A. We calculate straight-line distances from the published coordinates using the Haversine formula and round each edge weight to three decimal places. Importantly, these values are not actual Google Maps road or walking distances. The simplified model ignores road layout, barriers, traffic, and building entrances.

07 • Greedy procedure (1:20)
ON SCREEN: Read the pseudocode in logical groups, not line by line.

The greedy approach chooses the best immediate move available. For our assigned nearest-neighbor algorithm, we begin at a chosen location and mark it as visited. While unvisited locations remain, we compare the distances from our current location to those unvisited vertices and select the nearest one. We then mark that new location as visited and repeat. After all locations are visited, we return to the starting vertex. The algorithm returns the complete closed tour and its total distance. It is simple, but a locally shortest choice does not always produce a globally shortest tour.

12 • Optimality and complexity (1:00)
ON SCREEN: Be precise: Greedy is not generally optimal; optional exhaustive check only for this small dataset.

Nearest Neighbor is a heuristic and does not guarantee the globally shortest tour in every Traveling Salesperson Problem. With n vertices, the algorithm may inspect up to n minus one unvisited vertices in a step. Repeating those scans gives a time complexity of O of n squared, as stated in our assigned reference. Because our dataset has only five vertices, we also ran a separate exhaustive audit of the four factorial, or twenty-four, possible fixed-start tours. On this particular matrix, the minimum was also three point nine three seven kilometers. This supports optimality only for the given dataset, not as a universal property of Greedy.
